# Hook Grading Rubric

Every hook option gets graded A through F against this rubric before it is shown to the user. Grade each option individually and show the grade with a one-line reason that names the specific criterion it nailed or missed.

Version note: v1.1. Pressure-tested July 2026 against 18 real analyzed hooks from Sandcastles (watchlist outliers from 1.6x to 400.6x, global outliers up to 4,220x) plus verified platform display mechanics. Criteria 4 and 6 were revised based on that data.

## What a hook must do

A hook has one job: stop the scroll and earn the next three seconds. It does not need to explain, sell, or summarize. If it makes the right person pause and want the next line, it worked.

## The six core criteria

Score every hook against all six, regardless of layer.

**1. Scroll-stop power**
Does it interrupt the pattern of the feed? Something unexpected, bold, contrarian, urgent, or emotionally charged. Recency is a proven lever here: "[Authority] just announced/killed/deleted [thing]" hooks were among the highest outliers tested. If it looks and sounds like everything around it, it fails this.

**2. Clarity at a glance**
Can it be fully understood in about two seconds with zero rereading? Clever that requires decoding loses to clear every time.

**3. Specificity**
Numbers, timeframes, named outcomes, real details. "Get more leads" fails. "12 leads in 9 days without spending a dollar on ads" passes. This was the single strongest signal in the performance data: nearly every winning hook carried a concrete number or named detail ("one file," "three text files," "7 visitors a day to 150," "$80 million"). Vague is the most common hook killer.

**4. Curiosity gap OR instant recognition**
A hook must create one of two itches:
- Curiosity gap: it opens a loop only the content can close. How, why, what happened next.
- Instant recognition: the target person sees themselves so precisely that they stop out of "that's literally me." POV hooks and lived-situation hooks win this way with almost no open loop at all.
Either path passes. A hook with neither fails. Do not grade recognition hooks down for lacking a curiosity loop.

**5. Audience match**
Would the exact target person instantly know this is for them? A hook that speaks to everyone stops no one. Calling out the audience directly, naming their specific situation, or triggering recognition (see criterion 4) passes this.

**6. Believability through receipts**
Bold claims win, but only when a receipt is attached or clearly promised: a real event, a named result, a demonstrated proof, a shown outcome. "Google just killed SEO" works when the content delivers the actual announcement. "I made $50k in a week" fails without proof the content can show. The test is not "is this claim modest," it is "can the content back this claim up." Unbackable hype is clickbait, and clickbait burns the audience permanently.
Receipts are tiered, weakest to strongest: third-party stats < an unknown's testimonial < borrowed authority (a respected name's framework) < your own result < a named pattern of client results < live demonstration inside the content < visible audience size < a warm referral. A bolder claim needs a higher-tier receipt to pass this criterion, and stacking two or three tiers grades stronger than leaning on one. Full tier table: conversion/funnel.md.

## Grade definitions

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Passes all six criteria, and at least one is exceptional. Would stop the target person cold. |
| B | Passes all six solidly, but nothing about it is remarkable. Usable, not memorable. |
| C | Fails exactly one criterion. Do not show to the user. Revise using the revision playbook. |
| D | Fails two criteria. Rewrite from scratch rather than patching. |
| F | Triggers an automatic fail below, or fails three or more criteria. Cut it. |

## Automatic fails

Any one of these is an instant F no matter how well the rest scores:

- Throat-clearing openers: "Hey guys," "In this video," "I wanted to talk about," "So today," or any greeting before the hook. Zero of the 18 winning hooks tested contained any wind-up.
- Could-be-anyone hooks: swap in any other business or topic and the hook still works, which means it says nothing
- Unbackable claims: a promise the content cannot deliver or prove
- Requires context the viewer does not have yet
- The payoff word or idea is buried past the point where scanning eyes stop

## Layer-specific checks

Apply these on top of the six core criteria based on which layer the hook belongs to.

**Verbal hooks**
- The first three words carry the weight. Grade the first three words as their own mini-hook. Every winning hook tested landed its payload word inside the opening phrase.
- Written for the ear, not the eye. Must sound natural spoken out loud in one breath.
- No wind-up before the hook. The first frame of audio is the hook.

**Written hooks** (verified platform display numbers, July 2026)
- LinkedIn: roughly 210 characters show on desktop and 140 on mobile before "see more." Write the hook to be complete within 140 characters so it survives on every device.
- LinkedIn: keep the opening in a single paragraph. A paragraph break can end the visible snippet early, cutting the hook off before the fold.
- Instagram: captions truncate around 125 characters, and roughly 80% of viewers never tap "more." The first 125 characters are effectively the entire caption.
- Facebook: feed truncation lands around 280 to 477 characters depending on device, and posts under 80 characters earn the most engagement. Treat 80 characters as the target, not the limit.
- Front-load the payoff. The most compelling word or number belongs as close to word one as possible.
- No hashtags, no emojis, no links in the opening line.

**Visual hooks** (rules of thumb, least validated layer: revisit when real cover data is graded)
- Must read at thumbnail size on a phone. If cover text needs a squint, it fails.
- Cover text is six words or fewer.
- High contrast between text and background. Faces, motion, or an unexpected image beat stock-photo energy.
- Must not look like an ad. Polished-but-native wins over designed-and-ignored.

## How to apply this rubric in a session

1. Generate all options first, then grade in a single pass.
2. Show each option with its grade and a one-line reason naming the criterion.
3. Revise or cut every C, D, and F before presenting. The user only sees A and B options.
4. If fewer than three options survive at B or above, generate a new batch rather than lowering the bar.
